    Reading the map

    Buildings and roads are coloured by how close they are to a river: darker red is closest, amber is at the edge of the chosen distance. Grey is outside it. The shaded area is the exposure zone.

    How to read this

    Squares are 200 m. A hotspot is an area with an unusually large number of buildings within the chosen distance of a river, compared with the other exposed areas (Getis-Ord Gi*; strong = z of 3.3 or more, clustered = 2.6 or more).

    Households are not in any open dataset used here. Buildings are the proxy: an apartment block holds many households, a shed none. People come from WorldPop, which looks inflated in this valley, so use them to compare areas, not as a headcount.

    A hotspot means many homes close to a river. It does not mean the smell there is worse, and rivers differ, so check the river named on each row.

    Method and caveats

    What is assumed

    No odour distance. The distance is whatever you set. A fitted odour reach appears only if field observations were run through calibrate (see the dashed chips and contours); until then the map shows exposure at any chosen distance, not a claim about where the smell reaches.

    Distances

    Metres in UTM 45N, from each building footprint's edge or each 25 m road piece's midpoint to the nearest OSM river line. OSM may omit covered (culverted) reaches.

    Sources

    Rivers, buildings, roads: OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL), fetched in cached Overpass tiles. Terrain: Copernicus GLO-30. People: WorldPop 2020. Multipolygon buildings and footpaths are not included.

    Basemap and valley

    Satellite imagery is Esri World Imagery; Map is CARTO (follows the theme); Terrain is OpenTopoMap. All are free public tile servers and need an internet connection. The valley outline is the drainage basin of the valley floor, found by a watershed on the elevation model, so no rim height is assumed. Contours come from the same model. Major roads are OSM motorway to tertiary, valley-wide. The ridge-line joyplot is an optional overlay.

    Rivers are not alike

    This map gives every river the same distance rule because no measurement says otherwise. In reality flow, sewage load, covered (culverted) reaches and season change how a river smells, and only the named rivers are mapped. To stop assuming: By river in Exposure shows each river's own exposure and lets you look at one alone; each smell report stores the nearest river, and calibrate --by-river fits a separate reach for every river that has enough reports (rivers without enough are listed, not guessed). Measured water quality (stations.csv) is shown where supplied.

    Hotspots

    Hotspots use Getis-Ord Gi* on 200 m squares of exposed buildings, comparing each exposed square with the other exposed squares at the same distance. The place name on each row comes from OpenStreetMap's Nominatim reverse geocoder (only the hotspot centre is sent); if it cannot be reached the row shows coordinates. Households are not available; buildings are the proxy, and WorldPop people look inflated.

    Search and smell reports

    Search sends the text you type to a public geocoder (Photon, or OpenStreetMap Nominatim as a fallback); nothing else leaves the page. The location button asks your browser for permission only when pressed; your position is used on this page and is not stored or sent unless you submit a report (rounded to about 10 m). The result says how far the spot is from the nearest mapped river and whether it is inside the zone you chose. That is a distance, not a smell forecast. A report records the spot rounded to about 10 m (never the typed address), the Kathmandu time, and your yes/no. Reports are self-selected (people who smell something report more), so they show where the map disagrees with experience; they are not a random sample.

    A city can look beautiful and still smell of its rivers

    The roads are neat, trees are planted to green the living spaces, and the infrastructure is managed. But the water that runs through the city is polluted.

    Every walking passer-by, every daily commuter and every family living near a river breathes that foul odour, and many have built up a tolerance to it. Are they living the same quality of life as people far from the rivers? A river could be a beautiful backyard. With unmanaged banks and failing sewage systems, it becomes a health hazard. That is the reality of Kathmandu.

    We proudly show the snow-capped mountains and the mimosa-lined Durbar Marg. What the eyes miss is what the nose notices. A beautiful place should smell pleasant, and for people living along the river banks of the valley that is unlikely.

    This map shows the buildings and neighbourhoods near the rivers, and how many there are. Explore it, check your own address, and tell us whether it matches what you smell. Something has to be done to make Kathmandu beautiful not only for the eyes, but for the nose as well.
